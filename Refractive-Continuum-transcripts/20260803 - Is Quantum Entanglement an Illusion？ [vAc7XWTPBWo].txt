In the macroscopic world, physics is strictly local. For one object to affect another, a physical signal must traverse the space between them at a velocity bounded by the speed of light. In quantum mechanics, this boundary appears to vanish. When the polarization of an entangled particle is measured, it correlates with its distant partner instantly, even across thousands of kilometers. When these correlations are plotted across different detector angles, the resulting statistics follow a specific non-linear curve, the cosine squared of the difference between the angles. This empirical data, shown here, reaches a specific mathematical amplitude of 2.82.

This value is known as Tsirelson's bound, and it represents the maximum correlation allowed by quantum mechanics. Albert Einstein argued that this spooky action was impossible. In 1935, he and his colleagues proposed that particles must carry local hidden variables, a pre-programmed set of data designated as lambda, that determines the particle's behavior before it is ever measured. This established the central conflict of modern physics. Either particles carry these secret instructions, or reality is non-local, allowing objects to influence one another across space without a physical signal. In 1964, John Bell turned Einstein's philosophical argument into a rigorous mathematical test. He asked a single question.

What is the absolute algebraic limit of correlation if particles are truly independent entities? Bell defined the system's global expectation value as a statistical integral, treating the particles as independent scalar functions. This led to the CHSH inequality. It groups four distinct mutually exclusive experimental runs into a single theoretical value, S. Bell then factored these four independent runs into a single mathematical grouping. The math here is constrained by the nature of the detectors. Because the outputs are restricted to positive or negative one, the distributive property of algebra dictates that if one part of the equation reaches its maximum, the other part must be zero.

Because of this binary restriction, the absolute mathematical maximum for this classical equation is exactly 2.0. It cannot reach the 2.82 observed in quantum experiments. Scientists spent decades testing this limit, closing loopholes related to detector efficiency and communication. Experiments at Alain Aspect's lab, Delft and NIST, consistently confirmed that the physical data reaches 2.82, breaking Bell's limit. Since the physical data exceeds the 2.0 ceiling, the consensus in physics is that local realism, the idea of independent particles carrying fixed data, is mathematically impossible. One prominent defense of local realism was the field argument.

If the universe is a continuous field, rather than a collection of point particles, Bell's 1964 theorem might not apply. Bell's 1976 paper, The Theory of Local Beables, proved a continuous field is limited by the light cone. A mathematical boundary splits the field, keeping Alice's region separate from Bob's. This forces continuous fields into the same factorizable integral Bell used for point particles. As long as the field is assumed to carry a definite local state, a fixed lambda, the 2.0 ceiling remains an absolute limit.

This 1976 paper is a cornerstone of quantum foundations because it shows that simply replacing particles with waves does not bypass the mathematical restriction on local correlations. To maintain local realism within these constraints, a model must avoid the assumption of a fixed static state. The asymmetric metric tensor proposes that an entangled emission is not two severed objects, but a single continuous topological loop acting like a rotating corkscrew. In this model, the ends of the loop do not have fixed spin orientations. They are oppositely undefined, meaning they carry no static data before measurement.

Because the loop is constantly rotating its face, it naturally simulates all possible orientations following the Huygens principle of wave propagation. This dynamic rotation circumvents Bell's 1976 proof. Bell's math requires lambda to be a fixed determining state, but a constantly rotating undefined medium possesses no such static information. Without a static instruction set to integrate over, the algebraic framework Bell used to derive the 2.0 limit can no longer be applied to the field. In this view, detectors are not forcing a particle to choose a state. They are independent geometric boundaries sampling a rotating medium. Because the boundaries are independent, there is no need for instantaneous communication.

The statistical correlation is a result of how these independent samples are tallied after the experiment. By replacing static hidden variables with a rotating topological loop, this model suggests that local parts can behave independently without a preset global order. If the measurements are truly local and independent, we are left with a mathematical problem. How does the local geometry produce the 2.82 correlation? The discrepancy lies in how the CHSH equation treats the space where these measurements occur, an assumption known as the absolute space fallacy.

By grouping different experiments into a single bracket, Bell assumes the geometric space for the first run is identical to the space for the second. As this diagram shows, he attempts to subtract Bob's results at different angles as if they exist on a single flat one-dimensional ledger. This assumes that outcomes from different detector angles can be subtracted as simple scalars in an absolute coordinate system. However, in the asymmetric metric tensor model, the state of the system is a two-dimensional continuous geometric tensor, not a one-dimensional data tag. Bell's theorem treats these dynamic two-dimensional systems as if they are fixed within an unchanging one-dimensional coordinate space.

When a detector's angle is changed from B to B prime, the relative coordinate basis of the measurement space rotates. Tensor calculus requires that you cannot simply subtract values that were measured in different rotated reference frames. To compare these measurements, a geometric transformation matrix must be applied to translate the data between the rotated frames. When these required transformations are ignored, the math produces the 2.0 ceiling as a result of the coordinate mismatch. When the data is evaluated covariantly, accounting for the geometry of the rotating field, the local yields naturally result in the cosine squared correlation.

This allows the asymmetric metric tensor to derive the 2.82 Tsirelson bound entirely through local interactions. This suggests that the correlations of entanglement are not the result of non-local influence, but of the deterministic geometry of classical continuum mechanics.
